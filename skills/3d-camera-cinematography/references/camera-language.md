# Camera language for realistic 3D science films

This reference translates cinematography concepts into practical decisions for product and science
explainers. It is not a list of mandatory presets: select a shot because of what the viewer needs
to notice or understand.

## 1. Shot size and purpose

| Shot size | Typical use | Watch-out |
|---|---|---|
| Extreme wide / establishing | Place the product in a room, establish scale and context | Subject may be too small for a short-form screen |
| Wide / full | Show the whole product or a person using it | Keep silhouette and action unobstructed |
| Medium | Show interaction between person and product | Do not crop the hand/action that explains use |
| Close-up | Reveal a control, outlet, switch, vent or important component | Maintain enough context to identify the part |
| Extreme close-up / macro | Show surface, opening, tiny component or material behavior | Macro scale can mislead; provide a scale cue if needed |
| Detail insert | Isolate a single action or component | Match screen direction and action state at the edit |

A shot can combine a size and an angle, e.g. "close-up, three-quarter low angle". Avoid vague
labels such as "medium close-up" without describing the actual frame.

## 2. Viewpoint and projection

- **Eye-level**: neutral, familiar, useful for product use and human interaction.
- **Low angle**: gives an object presence; do not use it to imply unsupported size or power.
- **High angle**: clarifies layout and component relationships.
- **Top-down**: useful for planar layouts, exploded arrangements and step-by-step assembly.
- **Three-quarter**: often reveals front and side surfaces simultaneously; useful for product hero
  views and cutaways.
- **Profile**: clarifies flow direction, linear motion or layered structure.
- **Orthographic**: useful for technical diagrams and aligned comparisons; it removes perspective
  convergence and should be clearly distinguished from a photographic view.
- **Perspective**: natural spatial depth; use for cinematic product scenes and environments.

For a scientific mechanism, choose the view that exposes the relevant relationship, not merely the
most dramatic view.

## 3. Lens and perspective

Communicate focal length as a full-frame-equivalent reference unless the actual camera sensor is
known. Practical starting ranges (not rules):

| Lens family | Approx. full-frame equivalent | Visual tendency | Useful for |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Ultra-wide | 14–24 mm | Strong spatial expansion near camera | Establishing a room or dramatic near-field reveal |
| Wide | 24–35 mm | Broad context with visible perspective | Product in environment, moving through a space |
| Normal | 40–60 mm | Familiar proportions | Product hero, neutral close/medium views |
| Short telephoto | 70–105 mm | Tighter framing, less perspective exaggeration at matched framing | Detail, portrait-like product views |
| Macro / long macro | 90–120 mm macro is a common reference range | Close focusing with controlled detail | Small components and surface detail |

Important:
- Perspective is governed primarily by camera position; changing focal length and moving the camera
  to preserve framing changes perspective.
- Wide lenses close to a product can exaggerate near parts and distort proportions.
- Long lenses need more working distance and can flatten spatial separation at matched framing.
- Do not specify a lens number if it does not improve execution; use "normal perspective" and a
  measured framing description instead.

## 4. Camera movement vocabulary

| Move | Camera behavior | Best reason to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Locked-off | Camera does not move | Let a mechanism or comparison be the motion |
| Push-in / dolly-in | Camera physically approaches subject | Increase attention or reveal a detail |
| Pull-out / dolly-out | Camera retreats | Reveal context or relationship |
| Pan / tilt | Camera rotates from a fixed position | Follow action or reveal an adjacent area |
| Truck / slide | Camera translates sideways | Reveal parallax or compare layers |
| Orbit / arc | Camera travels around a subject while aiming at it | Reveal 3D form or show a component relationship |
| Crane / pedestal | Camera moves vertically | Reveal height, layers or spatial arrangement |
| Tracking | Camera travels with a moving subject | Preserve subject framing during movement |
| Rack focus | Focus shifts between depth planes | Redirect attention without moving the camera |
| Turntable | Subject rotates while camera remains fixed | Controlled product inspection; declare that the object rotates |

Do not call every move a "zoom". A zoom changes focal length; a dolly changes camera position and
therefore perspective.

## 5. Movement curve and duration

Describe movement as phases:
- **Ease-in**: begins gently, accelerates into the move.
- **Constant**: maintains speed.
- **Ease-out**: decelerates smoothly into the final composition.
- **Hold**: pauses so the viewer can inspect the result.

For a short explainer shot, a useful default is one dominant move with a clear beginning and end.
Avoid combining push + orbit + crane + rack focus unless each is necessary and can be executed
cleanly.

Use timing as an editorial tool:
- opening: reveal enough context before the camera closes in
- explanation: hold the camera still when the mechanism itself is complex
- transition: complete the move before the cut, or match motion across the cut
- detail: leave a readable hold after arriving at the focal point

Never allocate more camera action than the audience can understand within the shot's ≤15-second
duration.

## 6. Composition

Use a simple hierarchy:
1. Primary subject or mechanism
2. Secondary supporting object / hand / component
3. Environment and depth layers
4. Labels, arrows, captions and graphic overlays

Consider:
- rule of thirds as a starting point, not a mandatory grid
- centered/symmetrical framing for technical hero views or precise comparisons
- lead room in the direction of movement or gaze
- negative space for titles and subtitles
- foreground objects only when they add depth without obscuring the subject
- visual separation between subject and background through value, color, focus or lighting

For vertical 9:16:
- design composition in the final vertical frame from the beginning
- keep the key object and the critical mechanism near the usable central area
- avoid placing essential parts at extreme left/right edges
- leave subtitle and platform UI clearance at the bottom/top
- do not assume a horizontal master can be cropped without losing context

## 7. Depth of field and focus

- Use depth of field to guide attention, not to hide detail.
- For a scientific mechanism, keep all causally important parts acceptably sharp.
- Use rack focus only when attention genuinely transfers between two depth planes.
- State the focus target at the beginning and end of a focus pull.
- Avoid extreme bokeh on educational close-ups if it makes the mechanism unreadable.

## 8. Shot transitions and continuity

Useful cut relationships:
- **Match on action**: cut while a hand/object continues the same movement.
- **Graphic match**: match shape, axis or composition between product exterior and cutaway.
- **Eyeline / screen direction**: preserve where a person looks and where motion travels.
- **Movement match**: continue a pan, slide or rotation across the cut.
- **Scale progression**: wide → medium → close-up → macro when progressively revealing detail.
- **Cause/effect**: show the input before the mechanism and the output after it.

Avoid crossing the action axis without a motivated reset or establishing shot. Keep the product's
front/back and left/right orientation stable unless the shot explicitly rotates it.

## 9. Science-specific visual ethics

- Label illustrative internal views, invisible flows and conceptual cutaways as "示意" when needed.
- Never use camera perspective to make a part look larger/smaller in a way that changes the claim.
- If a component is enlarged for readability, preserve its relationship and mark the scale change.
- Distinguish actual product construction from a generalized typical structure.
- Use arrows, particles and color coding only with a clear legend and scientifically appropriate
  meaning.
- Do not show a heat/air/electric flow direction that contradicts the verified explanation.
